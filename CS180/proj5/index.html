Diffusion Models

Part A

Overview

This project explores diffusion models through hands-on implementation and experimentation. In Part A, we work with the pretrained DeepFloyd IF model to understand diffusion sampling, prompt conditioning, and image generation.

All experiments in this section were conducted using pretrained models, focusing on understanding diffusion behavior rather than training from scratch.

Part 1.1: Forward Diffusion Process

I implemented the forward diffusion process that adds Gaussian noise to a clean image according to the diffusion schedule. As the timestep increases, the image becomes progressively noisier.

Campanile at noise levels t = 250, 500, 750
Part 1.2: Classical Gaussian Denoising

I attempted to denoise the noisy Campanile images using Gaussian blur. This method struggled to remove diffusion noise without severely blurring the image, especially at higher noise levels.

Gaussian denoising at t = 250, 500, 750
Part 1.3: One-Step Denoising

I used the pretrained DeepFloyd Stage-I UNet to estimate and remove noise in a single denoising step. This produced significantly better results than Gaussian blur, especially for moderate noise levels.

Original Campanile
Noisy Inputs

Part 1.4: Iterative Denoising

I implemented iterative denoising using a strided timestep schedule. Compared to one-step denoising, this approach gradually removes noise and produces much higher-quality reconstructions.

Iterative denoising progression and comparisons
Part 1.5: Sampling from Noise

By starting from pure Gaussian noise and applying iterative denoising, I generated images from scratch using the prompt "a high quality photo".

Samples generated from pure noise
Part 1.6: Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG)

I implemented classifier-free guidance (CFG) by combining conditional and unconditional noise estimates. This noticeably improved image quality and structure compared to unguided sampling.

Samples generated with CFG
Part 1.7: Image-to-Image Translation (SDEdit)

I applied SDEdit by adding controlled amounts of noise to real images and projecting them back onto the natural image manifold. Increasing the noise level results in larger semantic changes.

Part 1.8: Visual Anagrams

I created visual anagrams by averaging noise predictions from two prompts, one applied to the image and the other applied to a flipped version. The resulting image changes meaning when rotated. The man_campfire and the galaxy_dog turned out quite well. When I was originally choosing my prompts, I choose a pencil and a rocketship to be my prompts for this question, thinking that similar objects would make a good anagram. Part 1.9: Hybrid Images

I created hybrid images by combining low-frequency components from one prompt with high-frequency components from another using factorized diffusion. Part B

Overview

In Part B, I trained UNets from scratch on MNIST. I first trained a single-step denoising UNet, and then trained a time-conditioned flow matching model that can iteratively generate digits from noise. I also trained a class-conditioned version to get more control and cleaner samples.

Part 1.2.0: Visualizing the Noising Process

I visualized the MNIST noising process by adding Gaussian noise with different sigma values. As sigma increases, the digits become much harder to recognize.

Part 1.2.1: Training a Single-Step Denoising UNet

I trained an unconditional UNet denoiser to map noisy MNIST digits to clean digits using MSE loss. The loss curve below shows the training progression.

Training loss curve for the denoising UNet

Part 1.2.2: Out-of-Distribution Testing

After training, I tested the denoiser on the same digit but with different noise levels sigma. It does best near the training setting, and as sigma gets very large it starts to lose structure and “guess”.

Clean digit
Denoiser outputs for different sigma values
Part 1.2.3: Denoising Pure Noise

I repeated the training procedure but changed the input to be pure Gaussian noise. After training, the model tends to output blurry digit-like “averages” instead of crisp diverse digits. This makes sense since MSE encourages predicting a kind of centroid of the training distribution.

Training loss curve (pure noise denoising)

These are the hyperparameters I used:

  • batch_size = 256
  • learning_rate = 1e-4

Part 2.2: Training a Time-Conditioned Flow Matching UNet

I added time-conditioning to the UNet and trained it to predict the flow from noisy x_t to clean x_1. The loss curve below shows that the model steadily learns the velocity field over training.

Training loss curve (time-conditioned flow matching)

  • num_epochs = 10

Part 2.3: Sampling from the Time-Conditioned UNet

I sampled digits by starting from noise and iteratively updating the sample using the predicted flow. The samples become much more legible as training progresses.

Sampling results at epochs 1, 5, and 10
Part 2.5: Training a Class-Conditioned Flow Matching UNet

I trained a class-conditioned model using one-hot digit labels with dropout (so it can still do unconditional generation). The loss curve below shows the training progression for the class-conditioned UNet.

Training loss curve (class-conditioned)

These are the hyperparameters I used:

  • batch_size = 64
  • learning_rate = 1e-2
  • num_epochs = 10
  • num_ts = 300
  • hidden_dim = 64
  • p_uncond = 0.1
  • guidance_scale = 5.0
Part 2.6: Sampling with Class Conditioning

I sampled with class-conditioning and classifier-free guidance. Compared to the time-only model, the digits are cleaner and it converges faster, and I can directly control which digit gets generated.
